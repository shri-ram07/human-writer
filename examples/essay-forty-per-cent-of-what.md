# Forty per cent of what?

"Precipitation: 40%," says the weather card on the phone. The US National Weather Service keeps a page that asks the two obvious questions about that number: will it rain 40 per cent of the time, or over 40 per cent of the area? The answer is neither. A 40 per cent forecast is a 40 per cent probability that the spot the forecast is for gets at least 0.01 inch of rain, about a quarter of a millimetre, in the period covered. Most people asked in surveys cannot give that definition, and the usual conclusion is that the public is bad with probability. The research points the other way. People cope with the probability. What they are almost never told is what it is a probability of.

The clearest evidence is old. In 1980 Allan Murphy, Sarah Lichtenstein, Baruch Fischhoff and Robert Winkler gave a questionnaire to 79 residents of Eugene, Oregon. The sample was small, and the authors discussed its limits, but the pattern was plain: people understood the probabilities well and misunderstood the event. Rain where? For how long? How much counts? The authors then drew the conclusion that matters to anyone who writes forecasts for the public. If the event is the source of the confusion, dropping the numbers will not reduce it. They argued for keeping the probabilities.

Twenty-five years later Gerd Gigerenzer and his colleagues asked pedestrians in Amsterdam, Athens, Berlin, Milan and New York what "a 30% chance of rain tomorrow" means. New York was the only city where most people gave the meteorologists' reading, that it rains on about three of every ten days like tomorrow. In each of the European cities that reading was judged the least appropriate of the options. The favourite answer there was rain for 30 per cent of the time, and the runner-up was rain over 30 per cent of the area. The authors' diagnosis fits in a sentence: a probability for a single event, with no stated reference class, can be read in several contradictory ways, so forecasters have to say which class of events the number belongs to. New Yorkers had probably not been taught better arithmetic. They had been hearing the format for longer, since the US Weather Bureau began sending probability forecasts to the public as a nationwide routine in 1965.

India makes a useful comparison. The India Meteorological Department's bulletins describe rain in words, and the words come with their reference class attached. The Imphal centre prints the definitions on its forecasts, and they count rain-gauge stations: "isolated" means rain at no more than 25 per cent of stations, "scattered" 26 to 50 per cent, "fairly widespread" 51 to 75 per cent and "widespread" more than 75 per cent. These are statements about coverage, the very reading the Europeans wrongly gave to a percentage. IMD's words are exact — about a different question from the one a person deciding whether to carry an umbrella is asking. A forecast of scattered rain over Konkan and Goa says how much of the subdivision will see rain. About one street in Andheri it says nothing at all.

Words, then, work only when someone has defined what they count, and the same holds outside weather. When Gigerenzer asked 160 gynaecologists how likely a woman with a positive mammogram is to have breast cancer, the most common answer was 90 per cent. The right answer is about 10 per cent. The doctors had been given conditional probabilities, a format that hides the denominator. Restated as counts, the problem is easy: of 1,000 women screened, about 103 test positive, and only 8 of those have cancer. After a one-hour lecture on turning probabilities into counts like these, 87 per cent of one group of 150 gynaecologists got the answer right, against 21 per cent before.

The fix for the weather card costs one line. Print the reference class beside the number: "Rain at this spot on 4 of 10 days like today." It is longer than "40%", and nobody who reads it will think it means rain over four-tenths of the city. Forecasters already know which class they mean. The 2005 study's recommendation was simply that they say it.

Until the apps print it, the confusion is a fault in the card.
